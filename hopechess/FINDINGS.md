# Hopechess bot: findings log

Game: [Tom1736/hopechess](https://github.com/Tom1736/hopechess) (snapshot in `upstream/`).
Both sides submit orders simultaneously; in 2v2 each team submits two
non-conflicting orders per round. See `upstream/README.md` for the rules.

## 1. Engine port

* `bot/cpp/engine.cpp` is a line-by-line C++ port of `upstream/engine.js`:
  move generation, simultaneous resolution (collisions, weaker-wins, king
  bounce, returning-king capture in 2v2, rest, promotion-before-combat,
  castling, en passant), forced passes, and repetition / 100-round draws.
* Exactness was checked by a differential test (`bot/tests/difftest.py`): 3,000
  random, heuristic and "quiet-move" games (175k rounds, 1v1 and 2v2) replayed
  through engine.js, comparing board, rest flags, hasMoved, en passant rights,
  counters, legal move sets and result every round. 0 mismatches. The test games
  covered 82 castles, 89 en passant captures, 521 promotions (277
  underpromotions) and 89 king meetings.
* Speed: about 0.25 µs per successor state without features. All joint actions
  of a 1v1 position (~900) take about 0.2 ms.
* Repetition detection keeps only the position hashes since the last
  irreversible round (a pawn move or capture). This matches engine.js's
  `positionCounts`, because no earlier position can recur after such a round.

## 2. Baselines (1v1, 100–200 games, colors alternate)

| match | result |
|---|---|
| upstream computer "hard" vs random | 100% |
| upstream "hard" vs "normal" | 50.5%; 96% draws, mostly threefold repetition: two deterministic bots shuffle |
| 1-ply Nash with tanh(material/scale) vs "hard" | 16–21% for every scale tried |

Takeaways:
* A **1-ply Nash** search with a bounded material eval is badly beaten by the
  upstream bot. The upstream bot scores king capture as ±1e6 and blends 35%
  worst case into its score, so it never leaves its king en prise. The bounded
  eval treats "king hunted by a queen" as only a little worse than losing
  material. Games are lost in king-vs-queen guessing chases (the Nash mix
  correctly randomizes, but the position is already lost).
  ⇒ The value function must understand king danger. This is what the learned
  value net is for.

## 3. Learning setup

* Network (`bot/model.py`): SE-ResNet trunk on 24 input planes (pieces, rest
  flags per color, castling rights, en passant targets, no-progress counter,
  repetition count, **2v2 flag**, ones). One network serves both modes. Heads:
  value (tanh, White's view) and policy for **both** colors (AlphaZero 73-plane
  move encoding, 2×4672 logits).
* Search (`bot/search.py`): depth-1 simultaneous-move matrix game. Candidates
  per side come from the policy prior (1v1: top-24 moves; 2v2: top-12 single
  moves → compatible pairs ranked by prior product, top-36), always adding
  "tactical" moves (king moves, moves into the enemy king's zone). Every joint
  action is resolved by the C++ engine and successors are valued by the net
  (exact values for finished games). The zero-sum matrix is solved with regret
  matching+ (800 iterations). We play by sampling our Nash strategy.
* A single search yields training targets for **both** sides: the Nash marginal
  of each side over single moves. Value target = 0.5·game result + 0.5·search
  value. Color-flip augmentation (mirror ranks and swap colors, negate value,
  swap and mirror policies).
* Inputs travel as an 80-byte compact encoding and are expanded to planes on
  the accelerator. This cut CPU time per searched round from 5.3 ms to 0.45 ms
  (1v1) and from 18.9 ms to 1.2 ms (2v2), and makes the replay buffer ~40×
  smaller.

## 4. Kaggle runs, round 1 (2× T4, 6 h each, 8 blocks × 80 channels)

Notebooks: `timsindelar/hopechess-rl` (A) and `timsindelar/hopechess-rl-2v2` (B),
generated by `bot/kaggle/make_notebook.py` (the code is packed into the notebook).
Logs: `bot/logs/r1_*.jsonl`. Models: `bot/models/r1_curriculum.pt`, `bot/models/r1_2v2only.pt`.

| run | data | games | positions | 1v1 vs hard* | 2v2 vs hard* |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: curriculum, 2v2 share 0 → 0.85 | 1v1 then mostly 2v2 | 73k | 2.95M | 0.60 | 0.78 |
| B: 2v2 only | 2v2 | 54k | 1.90M | 0.60 | 0.74 |

\*Average of the last four in-training evaluations (40 games each, colors
alternating, Nash sampling at temperature 1), so roughly ±0.04.

Observations:
* Throughput is GPU-bound: ~97k successor evaluations/s on two T4s. Network
  inference is about 85% of self-play time. A 1v1 searched round costs ~400
  evaluations and a 2v2 round ~900, so 2v2 self-play produces about half as
  many positions per hour.
* Within the first ~10 minutes the net already scores ~30% against "hard" in
  1v1. It reaches parity with "hard" after ~1M positions.
* **2v2 skill transfers to 1v1 and vice versa.** B never played a 1v1 game,
  yet it scores the same 0.60 in 1v1 against "hard" as A. A spent its first
  half almost only on 1v1 and was already at 0.44–0.71 in 2v2 when the 2v2
  share was only 20–40%. Both modes share one network with a mode flag.
* The 1v1 curriculum did not hurt 2v2 and gives more positions per GPU hour.
* Many 1v1 games against "hard" are draws (threefold repetition): the
  deterministic upstream bot shuffles, and our sampled Nash play often lets it.

## 5. Evaluation of round 1 (Kaggle `hopechess-eval`, 200 games per match, colors alternate)

| match | 2v2 | 1v1 |
|---|---|---|
| A (curriculum) vs B (2v2 only) | 0.568 ±0.035 (+47 Elo) | 0.512 (+9) |
| A vs "hard" | 0.818 (+260) | 0.632 (+94) |
| B vs "hard" | 0.843 (+291) | 0.665 (+119) |
| A final vs A at iteration 50 (half-way) | 0.835 (+282) | 0.677 (+129) |
| A + selective depth-2 (deepen=8) vs A | **0.640 (+100)** | **0.723 (+166)** |
| A at temperature 0.5 vs A (temperature 1) | 0.435 (−45) | 0.510 |
| A argmax vs A (temperature 1) | 0.510 | 0.517 |

* A slightly beats B head-to-head in 2v2, but B scores slightly better against
  "hard". Both differences are within ~2σ, so the curriculum is at least as
  good as 2v2-only training and we continue from A.
* Training had not converged: the second half of run A was worth +282 Elo in
  2v2 and +129 in 1v1. ⇒ round 2 continues training from A (lr 5e-4, 85% 2v2).
* **Search depth matters a lot at play time.** Re-searching the ~8 most likely
  joint actions (plus best responses) one round deeper gains +100 to +166 Elo.
  It costs ~5–10× more time per move (2–7 s on 4 CPU cores), so the live
  client uses it when the clock allows.
* Sampling from the Nash mixed strategy (temperature 1) is as good as or better
  than sharpening it, consistent with simultaneous-move theory. Greedy play is
  exploitable, and the search knows it.

## 6. Playing next to a teammate that is not the bot (2v2 single seat)

In real 2v2 rooms the bot may sit next to a human or another program. Test:
bot + upstream "hard" computer vs a team of two "hard" computers (local arena,
60 games each; reference: "hard" + "hard" scores 0.483).

| who submits first | bot's method | score |
|---|---|---|
| random order | team plan | 0.375 |
| bot always first | team plan (plays its half of a Nash pair) | 0.342 |
| bot always second | best complement to the teammate's reserved order | **0.633** |
| bot always first | expectation over teammate replies ~ policy prior | 0.317 |
| bot always first | same, quantal-response teammate (β=10), 100 games | 0.225 |
| bot always first | same as prior-expectation but **argmax instead of sampling**, 100 games | **0.565** |
| bot always first | team plan with argmax (control), 100 games | 0.340 |

With a teammate that *does* react to the reservation (an earlier checkpoint of
the bot, moving second), 100 games each:

| bot's first move | score |
|---|---|
| team plan (its half of a sampled Nash pair) | 0.665 |
| robust, expectation over policy prior, sampled | **0.755** |
| robust, quantal-response teammate (β=10) | 0.570 |

* Moving **second** with the teammate's order known is strong. Moving **first**
  with the team plan is harmful when the teammate does not complete the plan:
  the bot's half is often a move that only works together with its planned
  partner.
* The upstream computer ignores the reserved order completely: it scores its
  move as if it were the only mover. So it is a pessimistic stand-in for a
  human, who sees the bot's preview and reacts to it.
* **A lone first mover must not randomize.** Sampling from the mixed
  strategy is right when the whole team's pair is randomized jointly. A single
  sampled half-move next to an independent teammate is often a "bluff" that
  only works with its planned partner. Taking the argmax of the robust
  single-move game turned 0.32 into 0.57 next to the upstream computer.
  The agent now uses argmax for robust first moves (`solo_temperature=0`).
* The control (team plan + argmax: 0.340) shows that the gain comes from the
  robust valuation combined with argmax, not from argmax alone.
* Even when the teammate does react to the reservation, the robust first move
  (0.755) beats playing half of the team plan (0.665). The quantal-response
  model that assumes the teammate will cover is too optimistic (0.570).
* The client therefore (a) waits a few seconds for a human teammate's order
  (`--wait`) and complements it, and (b) only then moves first with the robust
  search. With `--teammate bot` (two bot clients on one team) the primary seat
  plays its half of the team plan at once and the second seat complements it.
  That second seat effectively reproduces full team play.
